# Voice — how everything written here reads

One voice for all of it: the tutorial, the demo narration, the README, patch notes, release notes,
menu wording, error messages, anything a person reads.

The name for it is **plain language, written in a conversational register and the second person** —
"friendly plain English". It is the register every software documentation style guide asks for.
Microsoft's calls for "warm and relaxed, crisp and clear"; Google's for "conversational and friendly
without being frivolous". Naming it matters because "make it friendlier" and "make it plainer" pull
in opposite directions unless you know which one you are aiming at. It is both, and these are the
rules that make it checkable rather than a matter of taste.

## The rules

- **Address the reader as "you"**, where it earns its place. "You'll find example patches in the File
  menu", not "Example patches may be found in the File menu."
- **Contractions are fine** — you'll, don't, it's. This is most of what makes a sentence
  conversational rather than stiff.
- **One idea per sentence.** The sentence ends when the idea does.
- **Name things outright.** "The ENGINE button", not "the control that starts the sound." Withholding
  the name to build a sentence around it wastes the reader's time twice: once reading the
  circumlocution and once working out what it meant.
- **Concrete verbs, active voice.** The cable goes; the fader sets; the button gates.

## The two failure modes

These are the ones that keep coming back, so they are rules and not preferences.

**No personification, and no scene-setting.** Things do not live anywhere, wait for you, stop
somewhere, or take you back. "Where the examples live" is "Example patches are in the File menu".
"Audio 1 taking you back" is "Audio 1 goes back to the modules". The related habit is the participial
fragment — "that cable running off the page", "Timbre folding the wave" — which has no subject and no
finite verb, and points at a scene instead of stating a fact. It reads as magazine writing. Say "The
cable goes off the page to the Mixer / Output tab."

**No enthusiasm.** No "simply", "just", "easy", "powerful", "beautifully"; no exclamation marks; no
telling the reader how they will feel about something. The friendliness is in the directness. Anything
described as easy is an insult to the reader who finds it hard.

## Instructions versus things to watch for

The tutorial's demonstrations come with a short **Watch for:** list, and it is not a set of
instructions — it is what to look at while the demonstration runs. Written as instructions, a
preamble tells the reader to do something they have not been shown yet, which is the wrong order.
Each item is a short declarative sentence about what happens, not an imperative.

## An example, three ways

> the two controls that make sound: ENGINE and the Master fader   ← withheld name, fragment

> ENGINE and the Master fader are what make the sound.            ← plain, but wrong: they do
>                                                                    different jobs

> The ENGINE button, above the mixer's output faders, gates the sound. The space bar does the same
> from anywhere.                                                  ← plain, correct, conversational
